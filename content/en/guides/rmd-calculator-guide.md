# Understanding RMDs: Don't Lose 25% of Your Retirement to the IRS

For decades, the government allowed you to grow your wealth inside a 401(k) or Traditional IRA completely tax-free. But the IRS doesn't wait forever to get its cut. 

Eventually, the government forces you to start withdrawing money from your retirement accounts and paying taxes on it. These forced withdrawals are called **Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs)**. If you fail to take your RMD, the IRS will hit you with one of the most punishing tax penalties in the entire tax code.

In this guide, we will break down the new SECURE Act 2.0 rules and show you how to use our free [RMD Calculator](/en/calculators/rmd-calculator) to stay out of trouble.

---

## 📅 The New RMD Age Rules (SECURE Act 2.0)

Historically, RMDs triggered at age 70½. However, recent legislation has pushed the starting age back, giving your money more time to grow tax-deferred.

**When do you have to take your first RMD?**
* If you were born **1951 to 1959**: You must start taking RMDs at age **73**.
* If you were born **1960 or later**: You must start taking RMDs at age **75**.

You must take your first RMD by April 1st of the year *following* the year you reach your RMD age. Every subsequent year, you must take it by December 31st.

---

## 💸 The Devastating RMD Penalty

What happens if you forget to take your RMD, or miscalculate the amount?
Under the new SECURE Act 2.0 rules, the penalty for failing to take an RMD is a massive **25% excise tax** on the amount you were supposed to withdraw (down from the old 50% penalty, but still brutal).

* *Example:* If your RMD for the year was supposed to be $20,000, and you forgot to withdraw it, the IRS will fine you **$5,000**.
* If you correct the mistake quickly (usually within 2 years), the penalty can be reduced to 10%.

---

## 🧮 How is an RMD Calculated?

Your RMD is calculated by taking your account balance on December 31st of the *previous* year, and dividing it by a "Life Expectancy Factor" published by the IRS in the Uniform Lifetime Table.

As you get older, your life expectancy factor goes down, which means the percentage of your account you are forced to withdraw goes up every single year.
