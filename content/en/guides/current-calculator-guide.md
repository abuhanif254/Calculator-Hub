# Electrical Current: The Flow of Electrons

If Voltage is the pressure pushing the water, **Current** is the actual water flowing through the pipe. 

In electrical engineering, Current is arguably the most important metric to monitor, because it dictates how thick your wires need to be, what size fuses you need, and whether a circuit is safe to touch. In this guide, we will explain the mechanics of current and show you how to use our free [Current Calculator](/en/calculators/current-calculator).

---

## 🌊 What is Electrical Current?

Current is the rate at which electrons flow past a specific point in a circuit. 

It is measured in **Amperes** (often shortened to "Amps" or represented by the letter **A** or **I** in equations). 
One Ampere represents one Coulomb of electrical charge (about 6.24 quintillion electrons) moving past a specific point in one second. 

* **Direct Current (DC):** The electrons flow in one constant direction (used in batteries and electronics).
* **Alternating Current (AC):** The electrons constantly vibrate back and forth (used in wall outlets and power grids).

---

## ⚡ The Dangers of High Amperage

High voltage isn't what hurts you; high current is. 

It only takes about **0.05 Amps (50 milliamps)** crossing through your heart to cause ventricular fibrillation (cardiac arrest). 
A standard household wall outlet in the US is capable of delivering **15 to 20 Amps** of current. This is why sticking a fork into an outlet is incredibly lethal—it delivers 300 times the lethal dose of current!

---

## 🔌 Wire Thickness and Current

As electrons flow through a wire, they bump into the atoms of the wire, generating heat. 
If you try to push 20 Amps of current through a very thin wire (like a headphone cable), the wire will instantly overheat, melt its insulation, and catch fire. 
This is why heavy-duty appliances like ovens and clothes dryers have massive, thick power cables. The thicker the wire, the more Amps it can safely carry without overheating.
