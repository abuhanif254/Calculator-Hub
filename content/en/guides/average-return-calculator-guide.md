# Average Return Explained: How to Measure Investment Performance

If your stock portfolio goes up 50% one year, and down 50% the next year, what is your average return? 

If you use simple math, you might think your average return is 0%. However, if you started with $10,000, a 50% gain takes you to $15,000. A 50% loss the next year takes you down to $7,500. You actually lost 25% of your money!

This is why measuring investment performance is tricky. In this guide, we will explain how to correctly calculate returns and use our free [Average Return Calculator](/en/calculators/average-return-calculator) to evaluate your portfolio.

---

## ➕ Arithmetic Average (The Misleading Method)

The Arithmetic Average is simply the sum of all your yearly returns, divided by the number of years.
* **Year 1:** +10%
* **Year 2:** -5%
* **Year 3:** +10%
* **Math:** (10 - 5 + 10) / 3 = **5% Average Return**.

While this is mathematically correct, it does not accurately reflect how much money is actually in your bank account, because it ignores the effect of compounding.

---

## ✖️ Geometric Average (The True Method)

Also known as the **Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR)**, the geometric average tells you the *exact, smoothed-out annual rate* your money grew at from the beginning to the end.

* If you started with $10,000 in Year 1.
* And you ended with $15,000 in Year 5.
* The Geometric Average calculates the exact percentage you would need to compound every single year to go from $10k to $15k in 5 years. 

This is the metric that professional investors and mutual funds use to report their performance. It is the only metric you should trust.

---

## 📊 The "Sequence of Returns" Risk

When you are withdrawing money from your portfolio in retirement, the *order* in which you experience returns matters just as much as the average.

If you have a million dollars and the market drops 20% in your first year of retirement, selling stocks to pay your bills will devastate your portfolio. Even if the market goes up 20% the next year, you have already sold those shares at the bottom—they aren't there to recover!
