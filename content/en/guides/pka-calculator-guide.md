# pKa Calculator: Measuring Acid Strength

Not all acids are created equal. If you spill vinegar on your hand, nothing happens. But if you spill sulfuric acid on your hand, you will suffer severe chemical burns! 

Chemists need a way to measure the *strength* of an acid. They do this using the **Acid Dissociation Constant ($K_a$)** and its logarithmic counterpart, **pKa**. In this guide, we will explain the difference between strong and weak acids, and how to use our free [pKa Calculator](/en/calculators/pka-calculator).

---

## 💧 Strong vs. Weak Acids

An acid's job is to release Hydrogen ions ($H^+$) into water. 
* **Strong Acids (e.g., HCl):** When they enter water, they dissociate (break apart) 100%. Every single molecule releases a Hydrogen ion.
* **Weak Acids (e.g., Acetic Acid):** When they enter water, they only partially dissociate. Maybe only 1% of the molecules actually release a Hydrogen ion.

To measure exactly *how much* an acid dissociates, chemists calculate the equilibrium constant, $K_a$. 
A large $K_a$ means a strong acid. A small $K_a$ (like $1.8 \times 10^{-5}$) means a weak acid.

---

## 🧮 What is pKa?

Just like with pH, $K_a$ values are often incredibly small, annoying numbers with lots of zeroes. To make them easier to read, chemists take the negative logarithm to find the **pKa**.

**The Formula:**
**pKa = $-\log(K_a)$**

Because of the negative logarithm, the scale is flipped!
* **Low pKa:** Strong Acid (easily gives up protons).
* **High pKa:** Weak Acid (holds onto protons tightly).

For example, Acetic Acid (Vinegar) has a pKa of 4.76. Hydrochloric Acid (Stomach Acid) has a pKa of -6.3!
