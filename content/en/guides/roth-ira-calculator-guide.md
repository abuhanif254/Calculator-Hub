A Roth IRA turns after-tax earned income into permanently tax-free retirement capital: you receive no upfront tax deduction, but every dollar of dividends, capital gains, and qualified withdrawals after age `59½` (once the account has been open at least `5` years) is `100%` exempt from federal income tax. Original owners also face zero Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs) during their lifetime.

For the **2026 tax year**, the IRS raised the base IRA contribution cap to **$7,500** (`$8,600` at age `50+` via the `$1,100` catch-up allowance), subject to Modified Adjusted Gross Income (MAGI) limits. ## Compounding Methodology and 2026 Statutory Rules

Roth IRA growth follows future-value compounding for an initial balance plus recurring deposits, with zero annual tax drag on dividends or rebalancing:

```text
Future Value (FV)    = P₀ × (1 + r/m)^(m×t) + PMT × [((1 + r/m)^(m×t) − 1) ÷ (r/m)]
Real Return (r_real) = [(1 + r_nominal) ÷ (1 + i)] − 1
Tax-Free Gain        = FV − Total Contributions
```

* **Variables:** `P₀` is starting balance, `PMT` is periodic deposit (`$625/month` equals the `$7,500` 2026 annual cap), `r` is annual return, `m` is compounding periods per year (`12`), `t` is years to retirement, and `i` is annual inflation.
* **Earned Income Rule:** Contributions cannot exceed taxable compensation (`W-2` or net `1099` income), though a non-working spouse can fund a Spousal Roth IRA on a joint return.
* **2026 MAGI Phase-Outs:** Direct eligibility phases out at **$153,000–$168,000** MAGI for Single/Head of Household filers, **$242,000–$252,000** for Married Filing Jointly, and `$0–$10,000` for Married Filing Separately living together.

## Step-by-Step Worked Example: Maxing Out at 2026 Limits

Assume a `35`-year-old starts with **$10,000** in an existing Roth IRA and contributes **$625/month** (`$7,500/year`) until age `65` (`t = 30` years) at a **7.5% nominal annual return** (`r/12 = 0.00625`, `n = 360` months):

1. **Growth of $10,000 Principal:** `$10,000 × (1.00625)^360 = $94,215.34`.
2. **Future Value of $625/Month Deposits:** `$625 × [((1.00625)^360 − 1) ÷ 0.00625] = $842,153.39`.
3. **Total Balance at Age 65:** `$94,215.34 + $842,153.39 = $936,368.73`.
4. **Total Capital Contributed:** `$10,000 + ($625 × 360) = $235,000.00`.
5. **Tax-Free Investment Gain:** `$936,368.73 − $235,000.00 = $701,368.73`.

In a taxable brokerage account facing `15%` long-term capital gains tax (plus annual dividend drag), that `$701,368.73` gain would lose over `$105,000` to federal taxes alone. Inside a Roth IRA, the entire `$936,368.73` remains spendable.

## Execution Strategies, Backdoor Roths, and Compliance Pitfalls

* **Backdoor Roth and the Pro-Rata Rule:** Earners above `$168,000` (single) or `$252,000` (joint) can contribute `$7,500` non-deductible to a Traditional IRA and immediately convert the balance to a Roth IRA. Watch IRS Form `8606` carefully: if you hold pre-tax balances in any Traditional, SEP, or SIMPLE IRA on **December 31** of the conversion year, the IRS treats the conversion as proportionally taxable across your combined IRA balance. Rolling pre-tax IRA balances into an employer `401(k)` (`$24,500` 2026 deferral cap) before year-end avoids this trap.
* **Five-Year Ordering Rules:** Direct Roth contributions can always be withdrawn tax- and penalty-free in any order; Roth conversions carry an independent 5-year clock per conversion before age `59½` to avoid a `10%` early recapture penalty; investment earnings come out last and require both age `59½` (or a statutory exception such as a `$10,000` lifetime first-home purchase) and `5` tax years since your first Roth IRA contribution.
* **Tax-Bracket Arbitrage:** Pair max pre-tax `401(k)` (`$24,500`) and HSA (`$4,400` individual / `$8,750` family) contributions during peak-tax years with a Backdoor Roth IRA to build tax-diversified retirement income streams. Model workplace growth with our [401(k) Calculator](/en/calculators/401k-calculator).
* **Excess Contribution Penalty:** Overcontributing past `$7,500` (`$8,600` age `50+`) or above your MAGI phase-out incurs a recurring `6%` annual IRS excise tax until you withdraw the excess plus net income attributable (NIA) before the tax filing deadline.
