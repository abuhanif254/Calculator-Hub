# Molar Mass: Reading the Periodic Table

If you need to mix exactly 1 mole of Water ($H_2O$) for an experiment, how many grams of water do you actually pour into the beaker? 

You can't answer this question without knowing the **Molar Mass** of the chemical compound. In this guide, we will explain how to read the periodic table, how to calculate the molar mass of any molecule, and how to use our free [Molar Mass Calculator](/en/calculators/molar-mass-calculator).

---

## ⚛️ What is Molar Mass?

Molar mass is simply the weight of exactly one Mole ($6.022 \times 10^{23}$ particles) of a specific chemical, measured in **grams per mole (g/mol)**. 

Because different atoms have different sizes (a Lead atom is much heavier than a Helium atom), a mole of Lead will weigh significantly more than a mole of Helium, even though they contain the exact same number of atoms!

---

## 🧪 Reading the Periodic Table

To find the molar mass of a single element, you just look at the Periodic Table. 

Underneath the letter symbol for each element, you will see a decimal number. This is the **Atomic Weight**. 
For example, under Carbon (C), you will see `12.011`. This means that exactly one mole of Carbon weighs 12.011 grams. Its molar mass is 12.011 g/mol.

---

## 🧮 Calculating Complex Compounds

What if you have a molecule made of multiple different atoms, like Water ($H_2O$)? 
You simply look up the atomic weight of each individual element and add them all together!

**Example: Water ($H_2O$)**
1. **Hydrogen (H):** Look at the periodic table. H = 1.008 g/mol. 
2. Because it is $H_2$, we have two Hydrogen atoms: `1.008 × 2 = 2.016 g/mol`
3. **Oxygen (O):** Look at the periodic table. O = 15.999 g/mol.
4. Add them together: `2.016 + 15.999 = 18.015 g/mol`.

The molar mass of Water is 18.015 g/mol. To get exactly one mole of water, you must pour exactly 18.015 grams of it into a beaker!
