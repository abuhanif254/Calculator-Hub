# Nernst Equation Calculator: Electrochemistry

Every battery in your home relies on a chemical reaction to push electrons through a wire. When a battery is brand new, it pushes electrons with a lot of force (high voltage). But as the battery dies, that force drops until it hits zero. 

To calculate exactly how much voltage a battery will produce at any given moment, chemists use the **Nernst Equation**. In this guide, we will explain the mechanics of cell potential and how to use our free [Nernst Equation Calculator](/en/calculators/nernst-equation-calculator).

---

## ⚡ Standard vs. Non-Standard Conditions

In a chemistry textbook, you often see the term **Standard Cell Potential ($E^\circ$)**. This is the voltage a battery produces when all chemicals are at exactly $1 \text{ Molar}$ concentration and the temperature is $25^\circ\text{C}$.

But in the real world, batteries don't stay at $1 \text{ Molar}$. As you use the battery, the reactants get consumed and the products build up. This changes the concentration, which changes the voltage! 

The Nernst Equation allows us to calculate the **Actual Cell Potential ($E$)** under these non-standard, real-world conditions.

---

## 🧮 The Nernst Equation

The equation looks intimidating, but it is just a way to subtract the "lost voltage" from the standard voltage.

**The Formula:**
**$E = E^\circ - \left(\frac{RT}{nF}\right) \ln(Q)$**

* **$E$** = Actual cell potential (Voltage).
* **$E^\circ$** = Standard cell potential.
* **$R$** = Universal gas constant ($8.314 \, \text{J/(mol}\cdot\text{K)}$).
* **$T$** = Temperature in Kelvin.
* **$n$** = Number of moles of electrons transferred in the reaction.
* **$F$** = Faraday's constant ($96,485 \, \text{C/mol}$).
* **$Q$** = Reaction quotient (Ratio of Products / Reactants).

*Shortcut:* At room temperature ($25^\circ\text{C}$), the messy constants simplify to: 
$E = E^\circ - \left(\frac{0.0592}{n}\right) \log(Q)$
