# Kb Calculator: The Base Dissociation Constant

While acids release hydrogen ions into water, bases do the opposite—they release Hydroxide ions ($OH^-$). Just like with acids, some bases are incredibly strong (like lye) and some are weak (like ammonia).

To measure exactly how strong or weak a base is, chemists calculate the **Base Dissociation Constant ($K_b$)**. In this guide, we will explain the chemistry behind $K_b$ and how to use our free [Kb Calculator](/en/calculators/kb-calculator).

---

## 🧪 What Does Kb Tell Us?

The $K_b$ value is a ratio of **Products over Reactants**. 
When a weak base ($B$) is put into water, it steals a proton from the water, creating a Conjugate Acid ($BH^+$) and a Hydroxide ion ($OH^-$).

* **Large $K_b$:** The base easily steals protons, creating lots of $OH^-$ ions. This means it is a relatively strong base.
* **Small $K_b$:** The base is weak and creates very few $OH^-$ ions. (e.g., Ammonia has a $K_b$ of $1.8 \times 10^{-5}$).

---

## 🧮 The Kb Formula

To calculate the constant, multiply the concentrations of the products and divide by the concentration of the leftover, unbroken base.

**The Formula:**
**$K_b = \frac{[BH^+][OH^-]}{[B]}$**

* **$[BH^+]$** = Molar concentration of the Conjugate Acid.
* **$[OH^-]$** = Molar concentration of Hydroxide ions.
* **$[B]$** = Molar concentration of the unbroken Weak Base at equilibrium.

---

## ⚖️ The Relationship: Ka * Kb = Kw

Because weak acids and weak bases are two sides of the same coin, their dissociation constants are mathematically linked!

If you multiply the $K_a$ of a weak acid by the $K_b$ of its conjugate base, the result will always equal the ion product constant of water ($K_w$, which is exactly $1.0 \times 10^{-14}$ at 25°C).

**$K_a \times K_b = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}$**

This means if you know the $K_a$ of an acid, you can instantly calculate the $K_b$ of its conjugate base!
