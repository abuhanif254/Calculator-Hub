The **5% rule** is a quantitative benchmark for comparing renting against buying based strictly on **unrecoverable costs**—expenses that permanently leave your net worth without building equity. Multiply a target home's purchase price by `5%` and divide by `12`: if you can rent a comparable home for less than that monthly figure, renting and investing the cash difference is likely the stronger financial move.

Calling rent "throwing money away" ignores the fact that homeowners also pay massive unrecoverable costs in the form of property taxes, structural maintenance, transaction fees, mortgage interest, and the opportunity cost of capital tied up in a down payment. Because those ownership costs scale proportionally with home prices, high price-to-rent markets often favor disciplined renters—especially over holding periods shorter than five years. ## Anatomy of Unrecoverable Costs and the 5% Rule Formula

An unrecoverable cost is any dollar spent on housing that yields zero recoverable asset value. For a renter, `100%` of monthly rent (plus renter's insurance) is unrecoverable. For a homeowner, only the **principal repayment** portion of the mortgage transfers wealth from cash into home equity; every other outflow is unrecoverable.

Financial researcher Ben Felix popularized the **5% Rule** to aggregate ownership's annual unrecoverable expenses as a percentage of total property value `V`:

```text
Annual Unrecoverable Cost = V × (t + m + c)
Monthly Rent Breakeven    = [V × (t + m + c)] / 12
```

- **`t` (Property Taxes ≈ 1.0%/yr):** Non-deductible or partially deductible municipal levies tied to assessed home value (ranging from `0.5%` in low-tax states to `2.2%+` in states like Illinois, New Jersey, or Texas).
- **`m` (Maintenance & CapEx ≈ 1.0%/yr):** Long-run annualized spending on roofs, HVAC systems, siding, appliances, and plumbing. Even in years with zero repairs, structural depreciation accrues silently.
- **`c` (Cost of Capital ≈ 3.0%/yr):** A weighted blend of after-tax mortgage interest paid to the lender on the borrowed `80%` and the foregone expected return (equity risk premium minus home appreciation) on the `20%` down payment.

Summing `1% + 1% + 3% = 5%` creates a baseline multiplier. In periods where 30-year mortgage rates exceed `6.0%` or HOA fees apply, substituting an adjusted `5.5%` to `6.0%` factor provides a more conservative reality check.

## Step-by-Step Worked Example: $500,000 Home vs. Comparable Rental

Suppose you are deciding between purchasing a `$500,000` single-family home (with `20%` down, `$100,000`) or renting an equivalent house in the same school district.

1. **Calculate Annual Unrecoverable Ownership Cost (Baseline 5%):**
   - Property Tax (`1.0%`): `$500,000 × 0.01 = $5,000/yr`
   - Maintenance Reserve (`1.0%`): `$500,000 × 0.01 = $5,000/yr`
   - Cost of Capital (`3.0%`): `$500,000 × 0.03 = $15,000/yr`
   - Total Unrecoverable Cost: `$25,000/yr`
2. **Compute the Monthly Breakeven Rent:** Divide `$25,000 / 12 = $2,083/month`.
3. **Adjust for High-Rate or High-Tax Reality (5.5% Check):** If local property taxes run `1.5%` or your mortgage rate sits at `6.75%`, raise the multiplier to `5.5%`: `($500,000 × 0.055) / 12 = $2,292/month`.
4. **Compare Market Alternatives:**
   - **If comparable rent is `$1,950/month`:** Renting costs `$133` to `$342/month` less in unrecoverable wealth—provided you invest the `$100,000` down payment plus closing cash into diversified assets.
   - **If comparable rent is `$2,900/month`:** Buying becomes structurally cheaper on an unrecoverable basis over a long horizon, while locking in fixed `P&I` payments against future rent inflation.

## Time Horizon, Behavioral Traps, and Tax Nuances

### The 5-to-7-Year Transaction Friction Rule

Even when monthly math favors buying, short holding periods destroy returns. Buying incurs `2%` to `4%` in loan origination, appraisal, title, and prepaid closing costs, while selling consumes `5%` to `6%` in brokerage commissions, concessions, and transfer taxes. On a `$500,000` home, `$30,000+` in round-trip friction wipes out early principal amortization if you relocate within 3 to 4 years.

### Critical Modeling Pitfalls

- **The "Disciplined Renter" Assumption:** Renting only outperforms when the cash saved on the down payment and lower monthly outflow is *actually invested* in productive assets—for example, maximizing 2026 tax-sheltered space (`$24,500` in a 401(k), `$7,500` in an IRA, or `$4,400`/`$8,750` in an HSA) and investing surpluses in broad index funds. If the renter spends the monthly savings on lifestyle inflation, the forced savings mechanism of a mortgage wins by default.
- **Rent Inflation vs. Fixed P&I:** A 30-year mortgage locks your `P&I` payment for 360 months while rents historically compound at `3%` to `4%` annually. Over a 15-to-30-year horizon, fixed debt payments shrink in real inflation-adjusted dollars while rent escalates.
- **Concentrated Leverage Risk:** Real estate returns benefit from `5:1` leverage (with `20%` down), amplifying gains during booms—but amplifying losses and illiquidity if you must sell during a localized downturn.
