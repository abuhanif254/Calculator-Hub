# Enthalpy Calculator: Heat in Chemical Reactions

When you burn a piece of wood, the chemical reaction releases a massive amount of heat and light. When you crack a medical cold pack, the chemical reaction instantly absorbs heat, freezing your skin. 

Where does all this heat come from, and where does it go? In chemistry, the total heat energy of a system is called **Enthalpy**. In this guide, we will explain how heat is transferred during chemical reactions and how to use our free [Enthalpy Calculator](/en/calculators/enthalpy-calculator).

---

## 🔥 Exothermic vs. Endothermic

During a chemical reaction, old chemical bonds are broken (which requires energy), and new chemical bonds are formed (which releases energy). 

The **Change in Enthalpy ($\Delta H$)** is the difference between the energy required to break the old bonds and the energy released by the new bonds.

1. **Exothermic Reactions (Negative $\Delta H$):** 
   * The new bonds released more energy than was required to break the old ones.
   * Heat is **released** into the environment. 
   * The reaction feels **HOT** (e.g., a campfire, an explosion).
2. **Endothermic Reactions (Positive $\Delta H$):**
   * The reaction required more energy to break the old bonds than it got back from the new bonds.
   * Heat is **absorbed** from the environment.
   * The reaction feels **COLD** (e.g., an instant ice pack).

---

## 🧮 Calculating Enthalpy Change

To calculate the total change in enthalpy for a reaction, you must subtract the total enthalpy of the original reactants from the total enthalpy of the final products.

**The Formula:**
**$\Delta H_{reaction} = H_{products} - H_{reactants}$**

*(Note: In chemistry, we use standard "Heats of Formation" tables to find the exact $H$ values for every molecule involved).*
