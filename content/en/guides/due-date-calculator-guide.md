# Due Date Math: How to Calculate When Your Baby Will Arrive

The moment you find out you are pregnant, the very first question is usually, "When is the baby due?"

While only about 4-5% of babies are actually born on their exact expected due date, having an Estimated Date of Delivery (EDD) is crucial for monitoring fetal growth and planning. In this guide, we will explore the math behind the dates and how to use our [Due Date Calculator](/en/calculators/due-date-calculator).

---

## 🧮 Naegele's Rule: The Standard Calculation

The vast majority of obstetricians and midwives use **Naegele's Rule** to estimate a due date. This rule is based on a standard 28-day menstrual cycle.

Instead of trying to guess the exact day of conception, Naegele's Rule starts from a date you are much more likely to know: the first day of your Last Menstrual Period (LMP).

**The Formula:**
1. Determine the first day of your LMP.
2. Add 7 days.
3. Subtract 3 months.
4. Add 1 year (if necessary).

*Example:* If your LMP started on November 1st, adding 7 days gets you to November 8th. Subtracting 3 months brings you to August 8th of the following year. Your EDD is August 8th!

---

## 🔬 How IVF and Ultrasounds Change the Math

Naegele's Rule assumes a perfect 28-day cycle with ovulation occurring exactly on day 14. If you have irregular cycles, this math will be slightly off.

1. **Ultrasound Dating:** In the first trimester, an ultrasound can measure the fetus's "Crown-Rump Length" (CRL). This is incredibly accurate, and doctors will often adjust your official due date if the ultrasound differs from the LMP math by more than a week.
2. **IVF Transfer Date:** If you underwent In Vitro Fertilization (IVF), the math is exact. The due date is calculated by adding 266 days (for a 3-day embryo) or 261 days (for a 5-day embryo) directly to your transfer date.
