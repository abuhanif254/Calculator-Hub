# Serial Dilution: Creating Precise Concentrations

While Molarity Dilution (M1V1 = M2V2) is used specifically for chemical moles, you will often need to dilute solutions where the concentration is measured in percentages, milligrams, or biological cells. 

In biology, medicine, and general chemistry, we use the generalized **Dilution Factor**. In this guide, we will explain how dilution ratios work, what a "serial dilution" is, and how to use our free [Dilution Calculator](/en/calculators/dilution-calculator).

---

## 💧 The Dilution Factor (Ratio)

The **Dilution Factor** is simply a ratio of how much stock solution you have compared to the total volume of your final mixture. 

For example, a **1:10 dilution** means:
* 1 part is your concentrated stock solution.
* 9 parts are the solvent (water).
* **10 parts total** (The final volume).

If your stock solution had a concentration of 50%, a 1:10 dilution reduces that concentration by a factor of 10. Your final solution will have a concentration of 5%. 

---

## 🔬 What is a Serial Dilution?

In microbiology, you often have a sample that is so densely packed with bacteria that you cannot count them under a microscope. You need to dilute the sample, but diluting it all at once might require an impossibly large bathtub of water!

Instead, scientists perform a **Serial Dilution**. 
This is a step-by-step process where you dilute the liquid, take a sample of that *new* liquid, and dilute it *again*. 

**Example of a 3-Step Serial Dilution (1:10):**
1. Take 1 mL of stock, add to 9 mL water. (Concentration is now 1/10th).
2. Take 1 mL of *that new mixture*, add to 9 mL water. (Concentration is now 1/100th).
3. Take 1 mL of *that new mixture*, add to 9 mL water. (Concentration is now 1/1000th).

By doing this, you can achieve incredibly weak, precise concentrations using very small test tubes!
