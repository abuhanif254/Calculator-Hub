# Heat of Reaction Calculator: Thermodynamics

Every chemical reaction either releases energy to the universe or steals energy from it. To figure out exactly how much energy is transferred during a specific reaction, chemists calculate the **Heat of Reaction ($\Delta H^\circ_{rxn}$)**.

In this guide, we will explain standard states, how to use standard heats of formation, and how to use our free [Heat of Reaction Calculator](/en/calculators/heat-of-reaction-calculator).

---

## 🌡️ Standard States

Because temperature and pressure drastically change how much energy a chemical holds, chemists agreed on a set of rules called the **Standard State**. 
When you see the little degree symbol ($^\circ$) next to a thermodynamic variable, it means the reaction was performed under standard conditions:
* Pressure = $1 \text{ atmosphere}$
* Temperature = $25^\circ\text{C}$ ($298 \text{ K}$)
* Concentration = $1 \text{ Molar}$

The **Standard Heat of Formation ($\Delta H_f^\circ$)** is the amount of heat absorbed or released when exactly one mole of a chemical is created from its pure, elemental building blocks under standard conditions.

---

## 🧮 Calculating the Heat of Reaction

To find the total heat of reaction, you must add up the heat of formation for all the products, and subtract the heat of formation for all the reactants.

**The Formula:**
**$\Delta H^\circ_{rxn} = \sum (n \times \Delta H_{f, products}^\circ) - \sum (m \times \Delta H_{f, reactants}^\circ)$**

* **$\sum$** = Sum (Add them all up)
* **$n, m$** = The stoichiometric coefficients from the balanced equation. (If the balanced equation says $2 H_2O$, you must multiply the water's heat of formation by 2!)
