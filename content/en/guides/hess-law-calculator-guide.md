# Hess's Law Calculator: Adding Up Enthalpy

If you climb to the top of a mountain, you will gain a specific amount of potential energy. It doesn't matter if you took a steep, direct path up the cliff face, or a long, winding path around the back of the mountain. You ended up at the exact same height, so your energy change is exactly the same!

This concept applies to chemical reactions too. In 1840, Germain Hess discovered that the total heat change of a chemical reaction is identical whether the reaction happens in one single step or in multiple smaller steps. In this guide, we will explain the mechanics of **Hess's Law** and how to use our free [Hess's Law Calculator](/en/calculators/hess-law-calculator).

---

## 🗺️ Enthalpy is a State Function

In chemistry, enthalpy ($\Delta H$) is a **State Function**. This means it only cares about your *starting point* (Reactants) and your *ending point* (Products). It does not care about the "path" you took to get there.

Because of this, if a complex reaction is too difficult to measure in a laboratory, you can calculate its heat by breaking it down into smaller, easier-to-measure "intermediate" reactions.

---

## 🧮 The Rules of Hess's Law

When adding intermediate reactions together to find a final $\Delta H$, you must follow two strict rules:

1. **The Reversal Rule:** If you reverse the direction of an intermediate chemical equation, you MUST flip the sign of its $\Delta H$. (If it was $+100 \text{ kJ}$, it becomes $-100 \text{ kJ}$).
2. **The Multiplication Rule:** If you multiply an intermediate equation by a coefficient (e.g., multiplying the whole equation by 2), you MUST multiply the $\Delta H$ by that exact same number.

Once you have manipulated the intermediate equations so that they add up perfectly to your target equation, you simply add all the manipulated $\Delta H$ values together!

**The Formula:**
**$\Delta H_{total} = \Delta H_1 + \Delta H_2 + \Delta H_3 ...$**
