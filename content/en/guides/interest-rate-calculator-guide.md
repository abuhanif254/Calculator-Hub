# Interest Rates Explained: Nominal vs. Effective APR

When you sign a loan document or open a savings account, the bank will heavily advertise a specific percentage rate. However, that advertised rate is almost never the *actual* mathematical rate you end up paying (or earning) over the course of the year.

To make smart financial decisions, you must understand the difference between nominal rates and effective rates. In this guide, we will break down the math behind compounding and show you how to use our [Interest Rate Calculator](/en/calculators/interest-rate-calculator) to reveal the true cost of debt.

---

## 🏦 Nominal Rate vs. Effective Rate (APR vs. APY)

### The Nominal Rate (APR)
The nominal rate is the "stated" or "advertised" interest rate. In the United States, this is often referred to as the **Annual Percentage Rate (APR)**. It is a simple interest calculation that ignores the effects of compounding. 
* If a credit card advertises an 18% APR, they are simply saying they charge 1.5% interest per month (18 / 12 = 1.5).

### The Effective Rate (APY)
The effective rate—often called the **Annual Percentage Yield (APY)** or Effective Annual Rate (EAR)—is the *true* mathematical rate you pay or earn after factoring in how often the interest compounds. 
* Because interest is added to your balance, and then you are charged interest *on that interest*, the effective rate is almost always higher than the nominal rate.

---

## 🧮 How Compounding Frequency Changes Your Rate

Let's look at how a seemingly innocent 10% nominal rate changes based on how often the bank compounds it.

**Formula for Effective Rate:** 
$r_{eff} = (1 + r/n)^n - 1$
*(where 'r' is the nominal rate and 'n' is the number of compounding periods per year).*

Let's apply this to a **10% Nominal Rate**:
1. **Compounded Annually (1x per year):** 
   * $(1 + 0.10/1)^1 - 1 = \textbf{10.00\%}$
2. **Compounded Monthly (12x per year):** 
   * $(1 + 0.10/12)^{12} - 1 = \textbf{10.47\%}$
3. **Compounded Daily (365x per year):** 
   * $(1 + 0.10/365)^{365} - 1 = \textbf{10.51\%}$

As you can see, a credit card that advertises "10% APR" but compounds daily is actually charging you over 10.5% a year!
