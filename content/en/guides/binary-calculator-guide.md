# Understanding Binary: How Computers Think

Every photograph, video game, and text message on your smartphone is ultimately stored as a massive sequence of 1s and 0s. 
But how can two simple numbers represent a 4K video? 

In this guide, we will demystify the Base-2 numbering system, explain why computers use it, and show you how to easily convert numbers using our free [Binary Calculator](/en/calculators/binary-calculator).

## 🔟 Base-10 (Decimal) vs Base-2 (Binary)

As humans, we use a **Base-10** numbering system (Decimal). We have 10 digits (0 through 9). When we count past 9, we run out of digits, so we add a new column to the left (the "tens" column), making the number 10. 

Computers use a **Base-2** numbering system (Binary). They only have 2 digits (0 and 1). When a computer counts past 1, it runs out of digits, so it immediately adds a new column to the left. 
* Zero = 0
* One = 1
* Two = 10
* Three = 11
* Four = 100

---

## 💡 Why Do Computers Use Binary?

Why don't computers just use our normal Base-10 system? 
Because at their core, computers are just billions of microscopic electronic switches (transistors). 
A switch can only be in one of two physical states: 
* **ON** (Electricity is flowing) = 1
* **OFF** (Electricity is blocked) = 0

It is incredibly fast, cheap, and reliable to build hardware that only has to detect whether voltage is present or not. If we tried to build a Base-10 computer, the hardware would have to perfectly distinguish between 10 different exact voltage levels, which would be error-prone and expensive.

## 🧮 How to Read Binary

In Base-10, our columns are: Ones, Tens, Hundreds, Thousands.
In Base-2, our columns double every time: Ones, Twos, Fours, Eights, Sixteens.

Let's look at the binary number **1011**:
* (1 × 8) + (0 × 4) + (1 × 2) + (1 × 1)
* 8 + 0 + 2 + 1 = **11**
The binary number 1011 is equal to the decimal number 11!
