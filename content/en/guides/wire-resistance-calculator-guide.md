# Wire Resistance: Why Cables Heat Up

We often think of wires as perfect tunnels for electricity, but the truth is that every wire fights back. This "fight" is called **Wire Resistance**, and it is the exact reason why your phone charger gets warm and why long extension cords can cause power tools to fail.

In this guide, we will break down the three factors that determine a wire's resistance, explain AWG (American Wire Gauge), and show you how to use our free [Wire Resistance Calculator](/en/calculators/wire-resistance-calculator).

---

## 📏 Factor 1: Length

The rule for length is incredibly simple: **The longer the wire, the higher the resistance.**

Imagine trying to drink a thick milkshake through a straw. If you use a normal 6-inch straw, it's fairly easy. But if you tried to drink it through a 50-foot straw, it would be almost impossible! The longer the tunnel, the more friction the fluid (or electrons) must overcome. 

If you double the length of a wire, you exactly double its resistance.

---

## ⭕ Factor 2: Thickness (Cross-Sectional Area)

The rule for thickness is the exact opposite: **The thicker the wire, the lower the resistance.**

Going back to our milkshake analogy, if you swap out a tiny coffee stirrer straw for a massive boba tea straw, the milkshake flows much easier. In electrical wiring, a thicker wire gives the electrons a wider path to travel down, drastically reducing traffic jams and resistance.

In North America, wire thickness is measured in **AWG** (American Wire Gauge). Counterintuitively, *lower* AWG numbers mean *thicker* wires.
* **14 AWG:** Standard household outlets (Thick, low resistance).
* **24 AWG:** Ethernet or USB cables (Thin, high resistance).

---

## 🧱 Factor 3: Material

Different metals hold onto their electrons with different strengths. 
* **Copper:** The gold standard for wiring. It has extremely low natural resistance and is highly conductive.
* **Aluminum:** Cheaper and lighter than copper, but it has roughly 60% more resistance than an equally-sized copper wire. If you use aluminum wiring, you must use a thicker gauge to compensate.
* **Gold:** While highly conductive and immune to corrosion, it is far too expensive for standard wiring and is usually reserved for microscopic computer chip connections.
