# P-Values: Is Your Data Statistically Significant?

If you conduct a medical trial for a new weight-loss pill and your patients lose an average of 2 pounds, how do you know if the pill actually worked? Could they have just lost those 2 pounds by sheer coincidence?

In statistics, we use **P-Values** to determine if the results of an experiment are actually meaningful, or if they just happened by random chance. In this guide, we will explain the Null Hypothesis and show you how to use our free [P-Value Calculator](/en/calculators/p-value-calculator).

---

## 🛑 The Null Hypothesis

Before any scientific experiment begins, researchers must establish a "Null Hypothesis." 
The Null Hypothesis is the boring, cynical assumption that **your experiment did absolutely nothing**. 

* **Your Hypothesis:** My new pill causes weight loss.
* **The Null Hypothesis:** Your pill does nothing; any weight loss observed is just random luck.

To prove that your pill actually works, you have to gather enough data to completely destroy (reject) the Null Hypothesis.

---

## 📉 What is a P-Value?

The P-value is a probability score between 0 and 1. It tells you the probability that your results happened by pure luck, assuming the Null Hypothesis is true.

* **A High P-value (e.g., 0.80):** There is an 80% chance your results were a total fluke. You have failed to prove your pill works.
* **A Low P-value (e.g., 0.02):** There is only a 2% chance your results were a fluke. It is highly likely your pill actually works!

---

## 🎯 The Magic Threshold: p < 0.05

In the scientific and medical communities, the agreed-upon threshold for "Statistical Significance" is usually **0.05 (5%)**.

* If your calculated P-value is **less than 0.05**, you are allowed to officially reject the Null Hypothesis and publish your paper claiming a successful experiment.
* If your P-value is 0.06 or higher, your data is not statistically significant.
