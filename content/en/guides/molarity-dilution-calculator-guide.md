# Molarity Dilution: The M1V1 = M2V2 Formula

When chemical supply companies ship acids to a university lab, they don't ship weak, heavily watered-down solutions. They ship highly concentrated "stock solutions" (e.g., 12.0 M Hydrochloric Acid) to save on shipping weight and space.

It is up to the chemistry student to dilute that dangerous stock solution down to a safe, usable concentration for their specific experiment (e.g., 1.5 M). To do this, chemists use the **Dilution Formula**. In this guide, we will explain how the math works and show you how to use our free [Molarity Dilution Calculator](/en/calculators/molarity-dilution-calculator).

---

## 🧪 The Golden Rule of Dilution

When you dilute a solution, you are only adding more solvent (water). **You are not adding or removing any of the actual chemical solute (the acid).** 

Because the number of moles of acid remains exactly the same before and after you add the water, we can use a very simple algebraic equation to find the missing variables.

---

## 🧮 The $M_1V_1 = M_2V_2$ Equation

The standard formula for calculating dilutions is:
**$M_1 \times V_1 = M_2 \times V_2$**

* **$M_1$ (Initial Molarity):** The concentration of your dangerous stock solution.
* **$V_1$ (Initial Volume):** How much of the stock solution you need to pour into the beaker.
* **$M_2$ (Final Molarity):** The weaker concentration you actually want for your experiment.
* **$V_2$ (Final Volume):** The total amount of liquid you want to end up with.

**Example Scenario:**
You need exactly 500 mL (0.5 L) of a 1.0 M acid solution ($M_2$ and $V_2$). The bottle in the storage closet is a highly concentrated 10.0 M stock solution ($M_1$). How much of the stock solution ($V_1$) do you need?

$10.0 \times V_1 = 1.0 \times 0.5$
$10.0 \times V_1 = 0.5$
$V_1 = 0.05 \text{ Liters}$ (or 50 mL).

You need to pour exactly 50 mL of the dangerous stock solution into a beaker, and then add 450 mL of water to reach the final 500 mL volume!
