# Inverters: Converting Battery Power to AC Power

If you are building a camper van, a solar cabin, or a backup power system, you will quickly encounter a major problem: your massive battery bank outputs 12-Volt **DC (Direct Current)**, but your laptop charger, TV, and microwave require 120-Volt **AC (Alternating Current)**! 

To bridge this gap, you need an **Inverter**. In this guide, we will explain how inverters work, how to avoid frying your electronics with the wrong type of wave, and how to use our free [Inverter Calculator](/en/calculators/inverter-calculator).

---

## ⚡ DC vs. AC: Why Do We Need an Inverter?

Batteries can only store **Direct Current (DC)**, where the electrons flow constantly in one single direction.
The power coming out of your house's wall outlets is **Alternating Current (AC)**, where the electrons violently vibrate back and forth 60 times a second (60 Hz).

You cannot plug an AC appliance into a DC battery; it simply won't work. An **Inverter** takes the flat DC voltage, electronically chops it up, and forces it to vibrate back and forth to simulate AC power from the grid!

---

## 🌊 Pure Sine Wave vs. Modified Sine Wave

When shopping for an inverter, you will see two main types. Choosing the wrong one can destroy your appliances.

**1. Modified Sine Wave (Cheap & Blocky):**
These inverters simulate AC power in a very clunky, blocky way. The power instantly jumps from positive to negative in jagged steps. 
* **Pros:** Very cheap. 
* **Cons:** Makes motors run hot and loud, causes static on TVs, and will permanently destroy sensitive electronics like CPAP machines and certain laptop chargers.

**2. Pure Sine Wave (Expensive & Smooth):**
These inverters perfectly replicate the smooth, rolling waves of power you get from the city power grid. 
* **Pros:** Safe for 100% of electronics. Devices run cool and quiet.
* **Cons:** More expensive.

*(Verdict: Always buy a Pure Sine Wave inverter unless you are on an extremely tight budget and only running simple lights or heaters).*

---

## 🛑 Sizing Your Inverter

You must size your inverter based on the maximum **Watts** you plan to pull at the exact same time. 
If you want to run a 1,000W microwave and a 50W laptop simultaneously, you need an inverter that can handle at least 1,050 Watts of continuous power. 

We recommend a **20% safety margin**, so you should buy a 1,200W or 1,500W inverter.
