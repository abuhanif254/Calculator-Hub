# How to Calculate Percent Error: Formula, Steps, and Examples

No matter how careful you are, experimental measurements in science and engineering are rarely perfect. There is almost always a discrepancy between the result you *measured* and the true, theoretical value.

**Percent Error** is the mathematical way of expressing how far off your measurement was, as a percentage of the true value. 

In this comprehensive guide, we will break down the percent error formula, explain why absolute value is critical, and show you how to use our free [Percent Error Calculator](/en/calculators/percent-error-calculator) for your lab reports.

---

## 🔬 What is Percent Error?

Percent error is a measure of inaccuracy. 
* A **high percent error** means your measurement was very far off from the accepted value. This could be due to faulty equipment, human error, or a flawed experimental setup.
* A **low percent error** (typically under 5%) means your measurement was very close to the true value, indicating a successful experiment.

---

## 🧮 The Percent Error Formula

To calculate percent error, you need two pieces of information:
1. **Experimental Value (EV):** The number you measured or calculated in your experiment.
2. **Theoretical Value (TV):** The true, accepted, or exact value (often found in a textbook or reference table).

**Formula:** 
Percent Error = | (Experimental Value - Theoretical Value) / Theoretical Value | × 100%

### Why the Absolute Value?
Notice the straight lines `| |` in the formula? Those denote **Absolute Value**. 
If you measure a piece of wood to be 9cm, but it is actually 10cm, your error is -1. 
However, in most scientific contexts, we only care about the *magnitude* of the error, not the direction. The absolute value strips away the negative sign. Being 1cm too short is a 10% error, just as being 1cm too long is a 10% error.

---

## 📝 Step-by-Step Calculation Example

Let's say you are in a chemistry lab trying to find the boiling point of a specific liquid. 
* You measure the boiling point to be **76.5°C** (Experimental Value).
* You look it up in a textbook, and the true boiling point is **78.2°C** (Theoretical Value).

**Step 1: Find the error.**
76.5 - 78.2 = -1.7

**Step 2: Take the absolute value.**
|-1.7| = 1.7
*(Your measurement was off by 1.7 degrees).*

**Step 3: Divide by the Theoretical Value.**
1.7 / 78.2 = 0.0217

**Step 4: Convert to a Percentage.**
0.0217 × 100 = **2.17%**

Your percent error is 2.17%. Since this is well under 5%, your experiment was highly accurate!
