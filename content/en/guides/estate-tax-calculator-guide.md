# Estate Tax vs. Inheritance Tax: What You Need to Know

When planning your financial legacy, taxes are often the biggest hurdle to passing wealth on to the next generation. Navigating the complex web of federal and state laws is daunting.

In this guide, we will break down the crucial differences between estate taxes (paid by the deceased's estate) and inheritance taxes (paid by the heirs), explain current federal exemptions, and show you how to use our free [Estate Tax Calculator](/en/calculators/estate-tax-calculator) to project your liability.

---

## 🏛️ The Federal Estate Tax (The "Death Tax")

The Federal Estate Tax is a tax on your right to transfer property at your death. It consists of an accounting of everything you own or have certain interests in at the date of death.

### The Lifetime Exemption
The most important concept in estate planning is the **Lifetime Exemption Amount**. The IRS allows you to pass on a certain amount of wealth completely tax-free. You only pay estate taxes on the amount that *exceeds* this threshold.

* As of 2024, the federal exemption is **$13.61 million per individual** (or $27.22 million for a married couple).
* If you die with a total estate worth $15 million, only the $1.39 million over the limit is subject to the estate tax.
* The federal estate tax rate is steep, typically scaling up to **40%**.

*Warning:* Unless Congress acts, this historically high exemption amount is scheduled to "sunset" at the end of 2025 and drop by roughly half!

---

## 🗺️ State Estate Taxes and Inheritance Taxes

Even if your wealth is below the massive federal exemption limit, you may still owe taxes depending on where you live.

### State Estate Tax
Several US states (such as New York, Massachusetts, and Washington) levy their own estate tax. These states often have much lower exemption thresholds than the federal government (sometimes as low as $1 million). 

### Inheritance Tax
An inheritance tax is fundamentally different. Instead of taxing the *estate* before money is distributed, it taxes the *heir* who receives the money.
* Only a handful of states (like Pennsylvania and New Jersey) have an inheritance tax.
* Spouses are almost always exempt, and direct descendants (children, grandchildren) usually face lower rates than distant relatives or friends.
