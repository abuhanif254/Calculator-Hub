# Understanding Inflation: How It Steals Your Purchasing Power

"A dollar just doesn't buy what it used to." We hear it all the time, and mathematically, it is absolutely true.

Inflation is the gradual loss of purchasing power of a currency over time, meaning it takes more money tomorrow to buy the exact same goods you bought today. In this guide, we will explain how inflation is measured and how to use our [Inflation Calculator](/en/calculators/inflation-calculator) to see its historical impact.

---

## 📈 How is Inflation Measured?

In the United States, the primary metric for inflation is the **Consumer Price Index (CPI)**, managed by the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS).

The government tracks a "basket of goods" that a typical consumer buys—groceries, gasoline, rent, medical care, and clothing. Every month, they check the price of this basket. If the basket costs 3% more this year than it did last year, the annual inflation rate is 3%.

### Why Does It Happen?
Inflation generally occurs for two reasons:
1. **Demand-Pull:** When consumers have more money to spend, but the supply of goods hasn't increased, people bid up the prices.
2. **Cost-Push:** When it costs companies more to produce goods (e.g., oil prices spike), they pass those costs onto the consumer.

---

## ⚠️ The Hidden Tax on Savings

If you hide $10,000 under your mattress for 20 years, you will still have $10,000 when you take it out. But because of inflation, that $10,000 will buy significantly less.

This is why investing is critical. To build wealth, your investments must grow at a rate *higher* than inflation. If inflation is 3%, and your savings account yields 1%, you are actually *losing* 2% of your purchasing power every year!
