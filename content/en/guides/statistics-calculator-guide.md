# Descriptive Statistics Explained: How to Analyze Any Dataset

Whether you are a student writing a research paper, a marketer analyzing campaign performance, or a data scientist exploring a new dataset, your first step is always **Descriptive Statistics**.

Descriptive statistics do exactly what they sound like—they describe and summarize the basic features of the data in a study. ## 📊 The Two Pillars of Descriptive Statistics

Descriptive statistics are generally broken down into two main categories: **Measures of Central Tendency** and **Measures of Variability (Dispersion)**.

### 1. Measures of Central Tendency
These metrics attempt to describe a whole set of data with a single value that represents the middle or center of its distribution.
* **Mean (Average):** The sum of all values divided by the total number of values. Best for symmetrical data without extreme outliers.
* **Median:** The exact middle value when the data is sorted. Highly robust against extreme outliers (e.g., used for household income).
* **Mode:** The most frequently occurring value in the dataset. Crucial for categorical data (e.g., eye color, favorite ice cream flavor).

### 2. Measures of Variability (Dispersion)
Knowing the center of the data isn't enough. You also need to know how spread out the numbers are.
* **Range:** The difference between the highest (maximum) and lowest (minimum) values. It gives a quick sense of the data's boundaries.
* **Variance:** Measures how far each number in the set is from the mean, and thus from every other number in the set.
* **Standard Deviation (σ):** The square root of the variance. This is the most important measure of dispersion because it is expressed in the same units as the original data. A low standard deviation means the data points tend to be very close to the mean.

---

## 📈 Population vs. Sample Data

When calculating Variance and Standard Deviation, you must know if your dataset represents a **Population** or a **Sample**.

* **Population:** You have collected data from *every single member* of the group you want to study (e.g., the grades of all 30 students in a specific classroom).
* **Sample:** You have collected data from a *subset* of the group to make estimates about the whole (e.g., polling 1,000 voters to predict a national election).

When dealing with a sample, the formula for variance is slightly modified (divided by $n-1$ instead of $N$) to correct for bias. This is known as **Bessel's Correction**.
