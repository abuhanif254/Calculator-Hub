# Understanding Income Tax Brackets: Marginal vs. Effective Rates

"If I get a raise, it will push me into a higher tax bracket and I'll end up taking home less money!" 

This is one of the most common—and completely false—myths about income taxes. In this guide, we will break down exactly how progressive tax systems work and how to use our free [Income Tax Calculator](/en/calculators/income-tax-calculator) to see your true take-home pay.

---

## 🏛️ How Progressive Tax Brackets Work

The United States (and many other countries) uses a **progressive tax system**. This means that as your income increases, the tax rate you pay on the *additional* income increases. 

However, moving into a higher tax bracket does **not** mean all of your income is taxed at that higher rate. Your income is chopped up into chunks (brackets), and each chunk is taxed at a specific rate.

### Example Scenario
Imagine a simplified tax system with two brackets:
* 10% on income from $0 to $50,000
* 20% on income over $50,000

If you earn **$60,000**, you do not pay 20% on the whole $60,000. 
* You pay 10% on the first $50,000 ($5,000).
* You pay 20% *only* on the remaining $10,000 ($2,000).
* **Total Tax:** $7,000.

---

## 📉 Marginal vs. Effective Tax Rate

To truly understand your taxes, you must know the difference between these two terms:

1. **Marginal Tax Rate:** The rate you pay on the *last dollar* you earn. In the example above, your marginal rate is 20%. This is the number people usually talk about when they say "I'm in the 22% bracket."
2. **Effective Tax Rate:** The actual percentage of your total income that you paid in taxes. In the example above, you paid $7,000 on $60,000 of income. Your effective tax rate is $7,000 / $60,000 = **11.6%**. 

Your effective tax rate is always much lower than your marginal tax rate!
