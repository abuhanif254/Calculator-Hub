# Ksp Calculator: The Solubility Product Constant

If you pour a spoonful of table salt ($NaCl$) into a glass of water, it instantly dissolves. But if you pour a spoonful of Silver Chloride ($AgCl$) into water, it just sinks to the bottom like sand. Why do some salts dissolve perfectly, while others do not?

To figure out exactly how much of a solid salt will dissolve in water, chemists use the **Solubility Product Constant ($K_{sp}$)**. In this guide, we will explain the chemistry behind solubility and how to use our free [Ksp Calculator](/en/calculators/ksp-calculator).

---

## 🧂 What is Ksp?

When a solid salt is placed in water, a tiny amount of it will break apart into ions. Eventually, the water becomes "saturated"—it cannot hold any more ions, and the rest of the salt remains solid.

This creates a dynamic equilibrium between the solid salt and the dissolved ions.
The $K_{sp}$ value tells you exactly where that equilibrium point is!

* **Large $K_{sp}$:** The salt is highly soluble (like table salt).
* **Small $K_{sp}$:** The salt is barely soluble (like silver chloride, which has a $K_{sp}$ of $1.77 \times 10^{-10}$).

---

## 🧮 The Ksp Formula

Because the original salt is a solid, its concentration doesn't change. Therefore, it is ignored in the equation! The $K_{sp}$ is simply the product of the dissolved ion concentrations.

**The Formula:**
For the dissolution: $AB_{(s)} \rightleftharpoons aA^+_{(aq)} + bB^-_{(aq)}$
**$K_{sp} = [A^+]^a [B^-]^b$**

*(Note: The concentrations are raised to the power of their stoichiometric coefficients).*
