# Voltage Drop: Why Long Wires Lose Power

Have you ever tried stringing together five 100-foot extension cords to run a power tool in your backyard, only to find that the tool barely spins? 
The tool isn't broken; it is simply suffering from **Voltage Drop**.

In this guide, we will explain why no wire is a perfect conductor, how distance destroys electrical pressure, and how to use our free [Voltage Drop Calculator](/en/calculators/voltage-drop-calculator) to ensure your next wiring project is safe and effective.

---

## 🛑 No Wire is Perfect

In basic physics classes, we pretend that copper wires are perfect conductors with zero resistance. 
In the real world, this is false. Every single inch of copper wire has a tiny amount of natural resistance. Over a distance of 1 foot, this resistance is microscopic and doesn't matter. But over a distance of 500 feet, that resistance adds up significantly!

As electricity travels down a long wire, it has to fight against this natural resistance. The longer the wire, the harder it has to fight. This fighting causes the electricity to lose pressure (Voltage). 

---

## 📉 The Consequences of Voltage Drop

If you start with 120 Volts at the house, but string together 500 feet of thin extension cords, you might only have 90 Volts left by the time the electricity reaches your power tool. 

If your power tool is designed to run on 120V, but it only receives 90V, it will compensate by trying to pull more **Current (Amps)** to make up for the lost power. Pulling too much current will cause the tool's motor to overheat, and it will cause the long extension cord to melt and potentially catch fire!

---

## 📏 How to Fix Voltage Drop

If you need to run electricity over a long distance, you have two options to reduce voltage drop:
1. **Increase the Wire Thickness (Gauge):** A thicker wire is like a wider water pipe. It offers less resistance, allowing the electricity to travel further without losing pressure. (In AWG, lower numbers mean thicker wire).
2. **Increase the Voltage:** High voltage travels much more efficiently than low voltage. This is why power companies use 500,000-Volt transmission lines to move electricity across the country!
