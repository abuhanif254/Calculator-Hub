# Electrical Resistance: Controlling the Flow

If you attach a tiny LED light directly to a 9-Volt battery, the LED will pop, smoke, and instantly die. The battery pushed far too much current through the LED. To prevent this, you need to add a **Resistor** to the circuit.

In this guide, we will explain the concept of electrical resistance, why certain materials are better conductors than others, and show you how to use our free [Resistance Calculator](/en/calculators/resistance-calculator).

---

## 🛑 What is Resistance?

Resistance is a measure of how much an object opposes the flow of electrical current. It is measured in **Ohms (Ω)**, named after Georg Ohm.

Think of it like a kink in a water hose, or a narrow toll booth on a busy highway. The higher the resistance, the harder it is for electrons to get through, which decreases the total Current (Amps) flowing through the circuit.

---

## 🧱 Conductors vs. Insulators

Not all materials are created equal when it comes to electricity.

* **Conductors:** Materials with extremely *low resistance*. Electrons can flow through them very easily. (Examples: Copper, Gold, Silver, Aluminum). This is why copper is used in household wiring.
* **Insulators:** Materials with extremely *high resistance*. They completely block the flow of electrons. (Examples: Rubber, Glass, Wood, Plastic). This is why power cables are wrapped in rubber insulation—to keep the electricity inside the wire and away from your fingers!

---

## 🔌 Why Do We Need Resistors?

Resistors are tiny components placed into electrical circuits specifically to *add* resistance. 

Why would we want to slow down the electricity? Because sensitive components (like LEDs or microchips) can only handle a very small amount of current. By placing a resistor in front of the LED, we can choke the current down to a safe level, allowing the LED to glow brightly without exploding.
