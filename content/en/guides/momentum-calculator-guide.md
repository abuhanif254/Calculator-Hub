# Understanding Momentum: The Physics of Collisions

If a mosquito flying at 10 mph hits your windshield, nothing happens. If a bowling ball flying at 10 mph hits your windshield, it shatters. 

Even though both objects were moving at the exact same speed, the bowling ball had vastly more **Momentum**. In physics, momentum is "mass in motion." In this guide, we will break down the formula, explain the laws of conservation, and show you how to use our free [Momentum Calculator](/en/calculators/momentum-calculator).

---

## 🚂 The Momentum Formula (p = mv)

Momentum is a vector quantity, meaning it has both a magnitude and a direction. 

**The Equation:** *p = m * v*
* **p:** Momentum (measured in kilogram-meters per second, kg·m/s).
* **m:** Mass of the object in kilograms (kg).
* **v:** Velocity of the object in meters per second (m/s).

Because momentum scales linearly with both mass and velocity, a massive object moving slowly (a freight train) can have the exact same momentum as a tiny object moving incredibly fast (a bullet).

---

## 🛡️ The Law of Conservation of Momentum

The most important concept in physics collisions is the **Law of Conservation of Momentum**. 
It states that in a closed system (where no external forces like friction are interfering), the total momentum before a collision is exactly equal to the total momentum after the collision.

If a moving car crashes into a parked car, the momentum isn't lost. It is transferred from the moving car into the parked car, pushing the parked car forward. The total combined momentum of both cars remains identical before and after the crash.

---

## 💥 Elastic vs. Inelastic Collisions

* **Elastic Collisions:** Objects bounce off each other perfectly without losing any kinetic energy to heat or sound (e.g., two billiard balls colliding).
* **Inelastic Collisions:** Objects stick together or deform upon impact. Kinetic energy is lost to heat and sound, but *momentum is still conserved* (e.g., two cars crashing and crumpling together).
