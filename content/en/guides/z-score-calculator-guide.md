# How to Calculate and Interpret Z-Scores in Statistics

Imagine you take two tests: Math and History. You score 85 on both. Did you perform equally well in both subjects? Not necessarily. If the class average for Math was 90, but the class average for History was 70, your History score is actually much more impressive.

To compare scores from different distributions mathematically, statisticians use the **Z-score** (also known as a standard score). In this guide, we will explore the Z-score formula, what the results mean, and how to use our [Z-Score Calculator](/en/calculators/z-score-calculator).

---

## 📊 What is a Z-Score?

A Z-score measures exactly how many standard deviations an element is from the mean (average) of its dataset. 
It allows us to standardize different datasets so they can be directly compared, mapping any normal distribution to a "Standard Normal Distribution" where the mean is always 0 and the standard deviation is always 1.

### Interpreting the Number
* **Z-Score of 0:** The data point is exactly average.
* **Positive Z-Score (e.g., +1.5):** The data point is 1.5 standard deviations *above* the average.
* **Negative Z-Score (e.g., -2.0):** The data point is 2 standard deviations *below* the average.

*Rule of Thumb:* In a normal distribution, 99.7% of all data points will have a Z-score between -3 and +3. Anything outside that range is considered an extreme outlier.

---

## 🧮 The Z-Score Formula

To calculate a Z-score manually, you need three pieces of information:
1. **$x$**: The specific data point you are evaluating (e.g., your test score).
2. **$\mu$ (Mu)**: The mean (average) of the entire dataset.
3. **$\sigma$ (Sigma)**: The standard deviation of the dataset.

**The Formula:**
$$Z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$$

*Example:* You scored 85 on a test ($x = 85$). The class average was 75 ($\mu = 75$), and the standard deviation was 5 ($\sigma = 5$).
$$Z = \frac{85 - 75}{5} = \frac{10}{5} = 2$$
Your Z-score is 2, meaning you scored 2 standard deviations above the class average.
