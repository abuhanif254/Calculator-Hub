# pH Calculator: Measuring Acidity and Alkalinity

When you drink lemon juice, it tastes incredibly sour. When you accidentally taste soap, it tastes bitter. This difference in taste is caused by the chemical properties of acids and bases. 

To measure exactly how acidic or basic a liquid is, chemists use the **pH Scale**. In this guide, we will explain the math behind the pH scale, how hydrogen ions work, and how to use our free [pH Calculator](/en/calculators/ph-calculator).

---

## 🍋 The pH Scale Explained

The pH scale typically ranges from **0 to 14**.
* **pH < 7 (Acidic):** Things like lemon juice, battery acid, and stomach acid. 
* **pH = 7 (Neutral):** Pure distilled water.
* **pH > 7 (Basic / Alkaline):** Things like soap, bleach, and baking soda.

But what does "pH" actually stand for? It stands for the **"power of Hydrogen"**. The pH scale measures the concentration of Hydrogen ions ($H^+$) floating around in a liquid. The more hydrogen ions there are, the more acidic the liquid is.

---

## 🧮 The Logarithmic Formula

The pH scale is not a linear scale like a ruler; it is a **logarithmic scale**. 

This means that a pH of 3 is not just "one step" more acidic than a pH of 4. It is **10 times more acidic**. A pH of 2 is 100 times more acidic than a pH of 4!

Because hydrogen ion concentrations are incredibly small numbers (like $0.0000001$ Moles per Liter), chemists use logarithms to turn them into easy-to-read numbers (like pH 7).

**The Formula:**
**pH = $-\log[H^+]$**

* **$[H^+]$** = The molar concentration of hydrogen ions.
* If $[H^+] = 1.0 \times 10^{-7}$ M, then $-\log(10^{-7}) = \mathbf{7}$. (Neutral water!)
