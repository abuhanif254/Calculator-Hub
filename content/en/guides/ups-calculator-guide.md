# UPS Calculator: Sizing Your Backup Battery

If the power in your house flickers for just one second, your TV will turn off, your desktop PC will crash, and you will lose all your unsaved work. 
To prevent this, IT professionals and home office workers use an **Uninterruptible Power Supply (UPS)**.

In this guide, we will explain how a UPS works, the difference between Watts and VA (Volt-Amps), and how to use our free [UPS Calculator](/en/calculators/ups-calculator) to properly size a battery backup for your electronics.

---

## 🔋 What is a UPS?

A UPS is essentially a massive surge protector with a heavy lead-acid battery hidden inside. You plug the UPS into the wall, and plug your PC and monitors into the UPS. 

When the power goes out, the UPS instantly switches to battery power (usually in less than 10 milliseconds). This switch is so fast that your computer doesn't even realize the wall power died! The UPS will beep loudly to warn you, giving you 5 to 15 minutes of battery life to safely save your documents and shut down your PC.

---

## ⚡ Watts vs. VA (Volt-Amps)

When shopping for a UPS, you will notice they are rated in **VA** (e.g., "1500VA / 900W").
* **Watts (W):** The Real Power your devices actually consume.
* **Volt-Amps (VA):** The Apparent Power. Due to inefficiencies in power supplies (Power Factor), devices pull slightly more current from the wall than they actually consume in Watts.

You must ensure that your total system load does not exceed **EITHER** the Watt limit or the VA limit of the UPS.

---

## 🛑 The "20% Margin" Rule

Just like buying a PC power supply, you should never run a UPS at 100% maximum capacity. 
If your desktop PC and monitors consume 400W total, you should look for a UPS that can handle at least **500W**. This 20% safety margin ensures the battery doesn't overheat during a blackout and gives you a few extra minutes of runtime.
