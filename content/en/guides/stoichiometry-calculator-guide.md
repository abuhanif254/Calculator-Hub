# Stoichiometry: The Math of Chemical Reactions

If you want to bake exactly 10 chocolate chip cookies, you need a recipe that tells you exactly how much flour and sugar to use. 

Chemistry works the exact same way. **Stoichiometry** is simply the "recipe math" of chemistry. It allows scientists to calculate exactly how much reactant they need to create a specific amount of product. In this guide, we will explain the core concepts of stoichiometry and how to use our free [Stoichiometry Calculator](/en/calculators/stoichiometry-calculator).

---

## ⚖️ Step 1: Balancing the Equation

You cannot do stoichiometry without a balanced chemical equation. The Law of Conservation of Mass states that matter cannot be created or destroyed. If you start a reaction with 4 Oxygen atoms, you must end the reaction with exactly 4 Oxygen atoms.

**Unbalanced Recipe:** $H_2 + O_2 \rightarrow H_2O$
*(We started with 2 Oxygens, but only have 1 at the end. Impossible!)*

**Balanced Recipe:** $2H_2 + O_2 \rightarrow 2H_2O$
*(We start with 4 Hydrogens and 2 Oxygens. We end with 4 Hydrogens and 2 Oxygens. Perfect!)*

The large numbers in front of the molecules (2, 1, 2) are called **Molar Ratios**. They tell us that we need exactly 2 moles of Hydrogen for every 1 mole of Oxygen to make 2 moles of Water.

---

## 🛑 Step 2: The Limiting Reactant

Imagine you want to build bicycles. You have 10 frames and 4 wheels. How many bicycles can you build? 
You can only build 2 bicycles, because you run out of wheels! The wheels are the **Limiting Reactant**. 

In chemistry, you almost never mix exact, perfect ratios of chemicals. One chemical will always run out first. The chemical that runs out first completely stops the reaction, dictating exactly how much product is made.

To find the limiting reactant:
1. Convert your starting grams of Reactant A into moles.
2. Convert your starting grams of Reactant B into moles.
3. Compare them using the Molar Ratio from the balanced equation to see which one runs out first!
