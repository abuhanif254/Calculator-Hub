To convert standard avoirdupois ounces (`oz`) to grams (`g`), multiply by the exact international constant `28.349523125`: `g = oz × 28.349523125` (often rounded to `28.35 g/oz` for everyday use). For precious metals weighed in troy ounces (`oz t`), multiply by `31.1034768` (commonly `31.1035 g/oz t`). For example, `4 oz` of butter equals `113.40 g`, while a `1 oz t` gold coin weighs `31.10 g`.

Whether you are scaling an artisan bread recipe on a kitchen scale, logging macros against metric nutrition labels, checking a parcel for postal rates, or valuing bullion, picking the right ounce standard matters. Below, we cover NIST and ISO definitions, three worked examples, a reference chart, and common weight-versus-volume mistakes.

## Exact Conversion Formula and International Standard

In the International System of Units (SI), the kilogram (`kg`) is the base unit of mass (ISO 80000-4), and `1 gram = 0.001 kg`. Under the 1959 International Yard and Pound Agreement—codified by NIST in Special Publication 811 and Handbook 44—the international avoirdupois pound was fixed at exactly `0.45359237 kg` (`453.59237 g`). Because one pound contains `16` avoirdupois ounces:

- `1 oz (avoirdupois) = 453.59237 ÷ 16 = 28.349523125 g` (exact)
- `g = oz × 28.349523125`

For precious metals and gemstones, commerce uses the **troy ounce** (`oz t`), defined as `480` international grains where `1 grain = 0.06479891 g` exact:

- `1 oz t (troy) = 480 × 0.06479891 = 31.1034768 g` (exact, or `31.1035 g`)

## Step-by-Step Worked Practical Examples

### 1. Baking: Scaling Flour and Butter to a Metric Scale

Suppose a US sourdough recipe calls for `18 oz` of bread flour and `4 oz` (one stick) of unsalted butter, and you want gram targets for your digital scale.

1. Convert flour: `18 × 28.349523125 = 510.2914 g`, which weighs out cleanly as `510 g` (or `510.3 g` for high-precision baking).
2. Convert butter: `4 × 28.349523125 = 113.3981 g`, commonly rounded to `113.4 g` or `113 g`.

### 2. Nutrition: Tracking Protein Portions

A meal-prep plan specifies `5.5 oz` of cooked chicken breast, while your calorie tracker logs portions in grams.

1. Multiply ounces by `28.349523125`: `5.5 × 28.349523125 = 155.9224 g`.
2. Enter `156 g` in your food log. Notice that using a sloppy `30 g/oz` mental estimate would yield `165 g`—overstating calories and protein by nearly 6%.

### 3. Postage and Precious Metals: Avoiding Standard Confusion

Consider weighing a `13 oz` padded mailer for postal rate thresholds versus verifying a `2 oz t` silver bar.

1. Postal parcel (avoirdupois): `13 × 28.349523125 = 368.5438 g` (`368.54 g`).
2. Bullion bar (troy): `2 × 31.1034768 = 62.2070 g` (`62.21 g`). Had you used the avoirdupois factor (`2 × 28.349523125 = 56.70 g`), your valuation would be short by `5.51 g`—nearly 10% of the metal's mass.

## Industry Use Cases, Regional Pitfalls, and Common Mistakes

### Weight Ounces (`oz`) vs. Fluid Ounces (`fl oz`)

The most common kitchen mistake is treating a fluid ounce (`fl oz`, a unit of volume) as interchangeable with an avoirdupois ounce (`oz`, a unit of mass). Only water near room temperature happens to weigh close to `1 oz` per US fluid ounce (`1 US fl oz = 29.5735 mL ≈ 29.57 g`, still 4.3% heavier than `28.35 g`). Dry sifted flour at `1 US cup` (`8 fl oz`) weighs roughly `120 g` (`4.23 oz`), whereas `8 fl oz` of honey weighs over `340 g` (`12 oz`). Never convert `fl oz` directly to grams without knowing ingredient density.

### Avoirdupois vs. Troy Ounces in Jewelry and Bullion

Precious metals dealers, refiners, and mints trade strictly in troy ounces (`31.1035 g`), while consumer kitchen and postal scales default to avoirdupois ounces (`28.35 g`). If you weigh a `1 oz` gold coin on a postal scale set to standard `oz`, it will read `1.097 oz` (`31.1035 ÷ 28.3495`), which often tricks sellers into thinking a coin is overweight or mislabeled. Switch the scale to grams (`g`) or troy ounces (`ozt`) before checking bullion.

### Nutrition Label Rounding (`28 g` vs. `28.35 g`)

On US packaged foods, FDA rounding guidelines display a `1 oz` serving as `28 g` and `2 oz` as `56 g` or `57 g`. When scaling bulk recipes or tracking macronutrients across large batches, use `28.349523125` rather than `28` to avoid `1.25%` under-reporting.

### International Postage Thresholds

Postal carriers enforce strict weight tiers (`20 g`, `50 g`, `100 g`, `250 g`, `500 g`) and ounce tiers (`1 oz`, `13 oz`, `16 oz`). Because `3.5 oz` (`99.22 g`) sits under `100 g`, rounding `28.35` down to `28` can misclassify a parcel once tape and labels are added.
