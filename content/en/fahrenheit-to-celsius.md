To convert degrees Fahrenheit (`°F`) to degrees Celsius (`°C`), subtract `32` from the Fahrenheit temperature and multiply by `5/9` (or divide by `1.8`) using the exact formula `°C = (°F − 32) × 5/9`. Because water freezes at `32 °F` instead of `0 °F` and each Celsius degree equals `1.8` Fahrenheit degrees, removing the `32`-degree offset first is essential for an accurate result.

United States weather reports, thermostat controls, and baking recipes rely almost exclusively on Fahrenheit, while virtually every other country uses Celsius under the metric system. Converting cleanly between the two prevents undercooked meats, collapsed cakes, and misinterpreted heat advisories.

## Exact Conversion Formula and International Standards

The exact conversion equation is `°C = (°F − 32) × 5/9`, which can also be written as `°C = (°F − 32) ÷ 1.8`. Unlike the Celsius-to-Fahrenheit direction, multiplying by `5/9` (`0.5555...`) often produces repeating decimals whenever `(°F − 32)` is not divisible by `9`. Retaining full fraction precision (`5/9`) before rounding at the final display step avoids cumulative truncation error.

Historically, Gabriel Fahrenheit established his scale in 1724, and it was later standardized around fixed physical points: `32 °F` for the ice point and `212 °F` for the boiling point of water at `101.325 kPa` (`1 atm`). Today, both NIST Special Publication 811 and the BIPM International System of Units define the degree Celsius (`°C`) as an exact offset from the SI base unit kelvin (`K`), with `0 °C = 273.15 K`. ISO 80000-2 governs the standardized mathematical notation and algebraic grouping rules used in technical conversions. Because the `180`-degree span between freezing and boiling in Fahrenheit corresponds to `100` degrees in Celsius, the exact conversion ratio reduces to `100/180 = 5/9`.

## Step-by-Step Worked Practical Examples

### Classic American Baking Recipe (350 °F to °C)

Most US cake, cookie, and casserole recipes specify `350 °F`. To set a metric kitchen oven accurately:

1. Subtract the freezing-point offset: `350 − 32 = 318`.
2. Multiply by `5`: `318 × 5 = 1,590`.
3. Divide by `9`: `1,590 ÷ 9 = 176.67 °C` (rounded to two decimal places).

On a metric oven dial marked in 5-degree or 10-degree steps, choose `175 °C` or `180 °C` for a standard top-and-bottom heating element, or `160 °C` if using a fan-forced convection setting.

### Summer Heat Advisory (95 °F to °C)

Suppose a US weather forecast warns of a `95 °F` afternoon high, and you are accustomed to metric temperatures:

1. Subtract `32`: `95 − 32 = 63`.
2. Multiply by `5`: `63 × 5 = 315`.
3. Divide by `9`: `315 ÷ 9 = 35 °C`.

Because `63` is an exact multiple of `9`, `95 °F` converts cleanly to `35 °C` with no rounding needed.

### Winter Cold Snap (14 °F to °C)

When converting a freezing `14 °F` overnight forecast:

1. Subtract `32`: `14 − 32 = -18`.
2. Multiply by `5`: `-18 × 5 = -90`.
3. Divide by `9`: `-90 ÷ 9 = -10 °C`.

Notice that subtracting `32` from `14` flips the intermediate number negative, producing an exact `-10 °C` reading.

## Industry Use Cases, Regional Pitfalls, and Common Mistakes

### Order-of-Operations Errors

The most common calculation mistake is dividing or multiplying by `5/9` before subtracting `32`. Evaluating `°F × 5/9 − 32` instead of `(°F − 32) × 5/9` produces severe errors—for example, `212 °F` would wrongly compute as `85.78 °C` rather than `100 °C`. Always keep `(°F − 32)` grouped inside parentheses.

### US Recipes vs. UK Fan Ovens and Gas Marks

US recipes assume radiant conventional ovens without continuous fan airflow, whereas many European, UK, and Australian home ovens default to fan-assisted ("fan" or "convection") modes. Converting `350 °F` mathematically yields `176.67 °C`, but using `180 °C` with a fan active often burns crust edges before the center cooks. Drop the converted Celsius setting by roughly `15 °C` to `20 °C` (`160 °C`) when baking on fan mode, and note that `350 °F` corresponds roughly to UK Gas Mark 4 (`180 °C` conventional).

### Food Safety and Meat Doneness Precision

USDA safe internal meat temperatures rely on exact Fahrenheit targets—such as `165 °F` for poultry (`73.89 °C`, commonly cited as `74 °C`) and `145 °F` (`62.78 °C`, rounded to `63 °C`) for whole cuts of beef, pork, and lamb. Rounding `165 °F` down to `70 °C` (`158 °F`) drops below safe pathogen-reduction thresholds, so always round food-safety Celsius targets upward when setting a probe thermometer.

### Temperature Differentials in HVAC and Engineering

When converting a thermostat swing, HVAC differential, or thermal expansion tolerance expressed in `Δ°F` rather than an absolute reading, omit the `-32` offset entirely. A `9 °F` indoor cooling drop equals `9 × 5/9 = 5 °C`, not `(9 − 32) × 5/9 = -12.78 °C`.
